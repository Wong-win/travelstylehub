<p>After years of painted white, lacquered grey and matte black, wood is back at the centre of interior design. Not wood-veneer cheapness or orange 1990s pine, but solid timber with visible grain and a finish you want to touch. The interesting part is not the trend itself but the way different species and finishes change the mood of a room.</p>

<h2>Oak: the reliable warm neutral</h2>

<p>Oak is the workhorse of warm interiors. It is hard, plentiful, and forgiving, with a grain that moves from subtle on rift-sawn boards to strongly figured on plain-sawn ones. Pale oak — especially white-oiled oak — lifts a room without making it feel cold. Smoked or fumed oak is darker and more dramatic, with a grey-brown tone that sits well against stone and linen.</p>

<p>For flooring or large furniture, white oak is the safer choice than red oak, which tends to run pink under warm light. If you are buying online, ask for a sample of the actual finish, not just the species.</p>

<h2>Walnut: depth without heaviness</h2>

<p>Walnut has a chocolate-brown heartwood that makes a room feel settled. It is softer than oak and more expensive, but a single walnut piece — a sideboard, a dining table, a bed frame — can carry a room on its own. The risk is overdoing it: an entire walnut room can feel like a 1970s boardroom. Pair it with pale walls, linen, and lighter woods.</p>

<p>Walnut also varies enormously between boards. A table made from the sapwood-rich edge of the tree will look very different from one made from the dark centre. Reputable makers show the actual piece or at least a representative sample.</p>

<h2>Finish changes everything</h2>

<p>The same board can look Scandinavian, farmhouse or mid-century depending on the finish. Matte hardwax oil keeps the wood looking raw and is easy to spot-repair. Lacquer gives a smoother, more uniform surface and more protection. High-gloss is back in some design circles but shows every fingerprint.</p>

<p>For a warm, natural look, choose a low-sheen oil or soap finish. Avoid orange-toned stains and heavy yellow varnish; both date quickly and flatten the grain.</p>

<h2>Mix species deliberately</h2>

<p>A common fear is that different woods will clash. They usually will not, provided they share a similar warmth and you repeat each one at least twice in the room. A walnut table with oak chairs works when the floor or a shelf echoes one of the two. The mistake is introducing five unrelated tones in small doses.</p>

<p>If you are unsure, keep the floor and largest pieces in one family, then use a second wood for smaller items. Metal and stone can act as a bridge between them.</p>

<h2>Let it age</h2>

<p>Wood changes. Cherry darkens, oak yellows slightly, walnut lightens in strong sun. This is not a defect; it is the reason solid wood feels alive in a way laminate never does. Use coasters, avoid leaving wet glasses on untreated surfaces, and re-oil every year or two depending on wear.</p>

<p>The return of warm wood is really a return to materials that improve with time. In a market full of disposable furniture, that is worth paying attention to.</p>
