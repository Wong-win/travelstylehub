<p>A rug is the fastest way to make a room feel finished — and the fastest way to make it feel wrong. Most rug problems are not about colour or pattern. They are about size and placement. Buy too small and the room looks like it is wearing a placemat. Float it in the middle of the floor and the furniture looks stranded.</p>

<h2>The living room rule</h2>

<p>In a living room, the rug should be large enough that at least the front legs of every seating piece sit on it. Ideally, all four legs of the sofa and chairs are on the rug, with a border of bare floor around the edges. This ties the furniture together into one zone instead of leaving each piece on its own island.</p>

<p>For a typical three-seat sofa plus two chairs, that usually means a 200 × 300 cm rug or larger. A 160 × 230 cm rug works in a smaller room with a two-seater. A 120 × 170 cm rug is a bedside rug, not a living room rug.</p>

<h2>Dining room: measure the chairs, not the table</h2>

<p>The rug must extend far enough that a chair pulled out for sitting still has its back legs on the rug. Add at least 60 centimetres to every side of the table. For a six-seat table, that often means 250 × 350 cm. If the room cannot take that size, consider no rug at all — a too-small dining rug is worse than bare floor.</p>

<p>Also check the chair legs against the pile. Thin legs on a thick pile will wobble; a flat-weave or low-pile rug is more stable.</p>

<h2>Bedroom: go wide</h2>

<p>A bedroom rug should extend at least 50–60 centimetres beyond each side of the bed so your feet land on something warm when you get up. Either place a large rug under the lower two-thirds of the bed, or use two runners on either side. A small rug at the foot of the bed is decorative, not functional.</p>

<h2>Material: match the traffic</h2>

<p>Wool is the default for good reason. It is durable, naturally stain-resistant, warm underfoot, and ages well. It is also the most expensive. Wool blends bring the cost down while keeping much of the performance.</p>

<p>Jute and sisal are inexpensive and hard-wearing, but coarse and difficult to clean deeply. They suit low-traffic areas and layered looks. Cotton is soft and washable but flattens quickly. Viscose and rayon feel silky but stain badly with water — avoid them in dining rooms and hallways. Synthetic polypropylene is practical for outdoor or high-traffic areas but has a shorter lifespan.</p>

<h2>Pile height and practicality</h2>

<p>Low-pile and flat-weave rugs are easier to clean and work under doors and chairs. High-pile rugs feel luxurious but trap dust, shed, and are hard to vacuum on hard floors. In homes with pets or allergies, lower is generally better.</p>

<p>Always use a rug pad. It stops slipping, protects the floor, adds cushioning, and can extend the life of the rug significantly. A pad slightly smaller than the rug is ideal.</p>

<h2>Pattern and colour</h2>

<p>If the room is calm, a patterned rug can be the focal point. If the room already has strong art or wallpaper, choose a quieter rug. Dark rugs anchor a light room but show light pet hair; pale rugs brighten a dark room but show dirt. There is no free option — choose the compromise you can live with.</p>

<p>Before buying, tape out the dimensions on the floor. A rug that looks generous online can look mean in a real room, and the difference between 160 cm and 200 cm is the difference between a room that works and one that does not.</p>
