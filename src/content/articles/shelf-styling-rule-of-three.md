<p>A styled shelf looks effortless and is almost entirely the result of a few repeatable decisions: what to group, what to lean, what to leave out, and where to let the eye rest. The shelves that look best in photographs are rarely the fullest ones.</p>

<h2>Start with books as structure</h2>

<p>Books are the backbone of a good shelf. They give weight, colour and height, and they make a room look inhabited. Group them in small stacks or vertical runs rather than packing a whole shelf edge to edge. A stack of four or five books with an object on top is one of the most reliable compositions in interior styling.</p>

<p>If the book spines are visually noisy, turn a few stacks spine-inward or remove the dust jackets. Keep the books you actually like — a shelf of untouched coffee-table books reads as decoration, not personality.</p>

<h2>The rule of three</h2>

<p>Objects grouped in odd numbers feel more natural than pairs. Three is the classic number: a tall object, a medium object, and a small one. Vary the height so the group has a silhouette rather than a flat top. Vary the material too — wood, ceramic, glass — so the eye has something to move between.</p>

<p>The rule is a starting point, not a law. Five objects can work if two of them are part of the same functional group, such as a set of candles. The underlying principle is contrast: if everything is the same height, scale or colour, the group disappears.</p>

<h2>Leave negative space</h2>

<p>The most common styling mistake is filling every square centimetre. Leave space above groups, at the ends of shelves, and between objects. Empty space is what makes the objects you have chosen look deliberate. If a shelf feels crowded, remove one thing and see how the rest improves.</p>

<p>A useful rule: each shelf should have one clear focal point and some breathing room around it. Two competing focal points on the same shelf will fight each other.</p>

<h2>Mix vertical and horizontal</h2>

<p>A shelf of all upright books looks like a library. A shelf of all stacks looks like a shop display. Combine both: a run of vertical books, a horizontal stack used as a pedestal, a leaning frame, a bowl. The changes in direction are what make the shelf interesting to look at.</p>

<p>Lean artwork or frames against the back of the shelf rather than hanging them. It adds depth and makes the display feel less formal.</p>

<h2>Repeat a material or colour</h2>

<p>Repetition creates coherence. If you have a brass frame, add a small brass object elsewhere. If you have a lot of white ceramics, repeat that across two shelves. The eye likes to find the pattern. Without some repetition, a shelf reads as a collection of unrelated things.</p>

<h2>Style for the room, not the photograph</h2>

<p>A shelf in a living room can carry more objects than a shelf in a bedroom. A kitchen shelf needs to survive steam and grease. A child's room needs lower shelves that can be reached. Style the shelf for how the room is actually used, then photograph it if you like. The lived-in version will always look better in person.</p>

<p>And when in doubt, take one thing off. The shelf will almost always improve.</p>
