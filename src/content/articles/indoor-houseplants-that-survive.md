<p>Most houseplants do not die from neglect. They die from too much water, too little light, and pots with no drainage. The romantic version of plant care — misting, talking to them, buying a special playlist — is far less useful than putting the right plant in the right corner and learning to leave it alone.</p>

<p>These are the plants that forgive the most, and the habits that keep them alive.</p>

<h2>Match the plant to the light you have</h2>

<p>Before buying anything, look at your room. Does it get direct sun for part of the day, bright indirect light, or only a weak glow through a north-facing window? Most houseplants sold as "low light" will survive in dim conditions but not thrive. A pothos in a dark hallway will live, slowly, with long gaps between leaves. The same plant near a bright window with filtered light will look completely different.</p>

<p>If your room is genuinely dark, choose snake plants, ZZ plants, or cast-iron plants. If you have bright indirect light, monsteras, rubber plants and peace lilies will do well. Direct afternoon sun will scorch many foliage plants; sheer curtains solve this.</p>

<h2>The easiest starter plants</h2>

<p><strong>Pothos</strong> tolerates low light, irregular watering, and almost any pot. It trails, so it works on a shelf or in a hanging planter. <strong>Snake plant</strong> prefers to dry out completely between waterings and can handle a surprising amount of neglect. <strong>ZZ plant</strong> stores water in its rhizomes and is ideal for people who travel. <strong>Spider plant</strong> is forgiving, easy to propagate, and tells you when it needs water by drooping slightly.</p>

<p>Avoid calatheas, ferns and fiddle-leaf figs as first plants unless you enjoy a challenge. They are beautiful, but they punish inconsistency.</p>

<h2>Water less than you think</h2>

<p>The single most common cause of houseplant death is overwatering. Roots need air, and constantly wet soil suffocates them. Water when the top two or three centimetres of soil are dry, not on a fixed weekly schedule. Stick a finger in, or lift the pot and feel its weight.</p>

<p>Always use a pot with a drainage hole, and empty the saucer after watering. If you love a decorative pot with no hole, keep the plant in a plastic nursery pot inside it and remove it to water.</p>

<h2>Repot with restraint</h2>

<p>Plants do not need a bigger pot every year. Repot when roots are circling the bottom or emerging from the drainage hole, usually every one to three years. Choose a pot only a few centimetres wider than the current one. Too much fresh soil around a small root ball holds moisture the plant cannot use.</p>

<p>Use a proper potting mix rather than garden soil. Most houseplant mixes include perlite or bark to keep the roots aerated.</p>

<h2>Clean leaves and check for pests</h2>

<p>Dust blocks light. Wipe large leaves with a damp cloth every few weeks. Inspect the undersides of leaves when you water — small webs, sticky residue or tiny moving specks mean pests. A shower and a dab of insecticidal soap will handle most early infestations.</p>

<h2>Placement is design, too</h2>

<p>A plant should earn its position. A tall plant can soften a bare corner or screen an awkward cable. A trailing plant can break up a shelf of books. Group plants in odd numbers and vary heights so the arrangement looks collected rather than lined up.</p>

<p>And if a plant dies, it is not a moral failure. Some rooms are simply better suited to a snake plant than a fern. Work with the light you have, and the plants will do the decorating for you.</p>
