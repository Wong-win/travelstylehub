<p>Most rooms are lit by one ceiling light and a lot of hope. The result is flat: everything evenly bright, no shadows, no warmth, no reason for the eye to move. Designers do not solve this by adding more light. They solve it by layering three kinds of light and giving each one a job.</p>

<h2>Layer one: ambient light</h2>

<p>Ambient light is the general wash that lets you move around safely — the ceiling fixture, a pair of ceiling spotlights, or a large pendant. It should be dimmable and it should never be the only thing switched on in the evening.</p>

<p>If you have a single central fixture, do not replace it immediately. Put it on a dimmer first. Being able to take it down to 20 percent changes the atmosphere more than a new shade will.</p>

<h2>Layer two: task light</h2>

<p>Task light is for reading, cooking, working, and seeing what you are doing. A floor lamp beside the sofa, a swing-arm lamp over a chair, a focused pendant over the kitchen counter, a bedside light you can angle. This layer is what makes a room usable after dark.</p>

<p>Choose the height carefully. A reading lamp should sit roughly at shoulder height when you are seated, with the shade below eye level so the bulb does not glare. If you can see the bare bulb from your chair, it is too high or the shade is too shallow.</p>

<h2>Layer three: accent light</h2>

<p>Accent light is the layer that makes a room feel designed. It picks out a wall, a plant, a picture, or a corner. A picture light above a print, a small uplight behind a plant, a slim sconce on a dark wall, a candle on the mantel. It adds depth, because it creates light and shadow rather than flattening everything.</p>

<p>Accent lighting is also the cheapest layer to add. One warm plug-in sconce or a rechargeable table lamp in a dark corner will do more than doubling the wattage of the ceiling bulb.</p>

<h2>Colour temperature: keep it warm and consistent</h2>

<p>Look for bulbs around 2700K in living spaces and bedrooms. Cool white bulbs (4000K and above) are for garages, offices and utility rooms; in a living room they make wood look grey and skin look tired. Also make sure all the bulbs in one sightline match. A warm lamp beside a cool ceiling light is the fastest way to make a room feel unfinished.</p>

<p>Warm dimming bulbs are worth the small premium. They shift from a clean warm white down to a candle-like amber as you dim them, which is exactly the transition you want between dinner and late evening.</p>

<h2>Placement: light the walls, not the air</h2>

<p>A useful rule is to light surfaces rather than the middle of the room. Light bounced off a pale wall or ceiling spreads softly and makes the space feel larger. A lamp pointed at a dark corner will lift it. A downlight aimed at the centre of the floor will not.</p>

<p>Try to avoid placing lamps directly in front of a window at night; the dark glass becomes a mirror and the effect is unsettling. Pull lamps slightly to the side so the window remains part of the composition.</p>

<h2>A simple upgrade plan</h2>

<p>If you want to improve a room this weekend, do it in this order. One: put the main ceiling light on a dimmer. Two: add a floor lamp beside the main seating. Three: add one small accent light in the darkest corner. Four: replace every bulb in the room with the same warm white. None of these steps requires an electrician, and together they will change the room more than a new sofa would.</p>

<p>Good lighting is not decoration. It is the difference between a room you use and a room you avoid after sunset.</p>
