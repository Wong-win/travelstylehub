<p>Istanbul, capital of two empires and cradle of centuries of history, offers a multitude of treasures to discover. Whether you are passionate about Ottoman history, Turkish cuisine, contemporary culture, or the Byzantine past, this city has something for every traveler. Here are the 16 best things to do in Istanbul to explore its many facets.</p>

<p>What makes Istanbul unusual is that its history is not confined to museums. Byzantine cisterns sit beneath ordinary streets, Ottoman palaces line the Bosphorus, and the call to prayer still marks the rhythm of the day across the old city. The city is split by water — the Golden Horn to the west and the Bosphorus to the east — and the most memorable days are the ones where you cross between the European and Asian shores at least once.</p>

<p>Allow at least three full days. Two days in Sultanahmet and Beyoğlu cover the headline monuments; a third day in Kadıköy shows you the city locals actually live in, and a fourth lets you take a ferry and slow down.</p>

<h2>1. Experience the extravagance of Topkapı Palace</h2>

<p>Topkapı Palace, residence of the Ottoman sultans between 1465 and 1830, is an opulent complex that reveals the secrets of the seraglio. Admire the Harem, the Imperial Council Chamber, the Imperial Treasury, and the Marble Terrace. The adjacent Gülhane Park, once part of the palace gardens, is now a magnificent public park.</p>

<p>The palace is really a small city: courtyards, kitchens, pavilions, and gardens arranged over a sloping site with views across the Golden Horn and the Bosphorus. The Treasury holds some of the most famous objects in the country, including jewel-encrusted daggers and thrones, while the Harem requires a separate ticket and is worth the extra cost for the tiled corridors alone.</p>

<p>Go early. The palace is the single busiest site in the city, and the courtyards fill quickly after ten in the morning. Combine the visit with Gülhane Park, which is free and a good place to sit after the crowds.</p>

<h2>2. Admire the majestic Hagia Sophia</h2>

<p>Built by Emperor Justinian in the 6th century, the Byzantine basilica of Hagia Sophia is renowned for its immense dome and golden mosaics. Since its reconversion into a mosque in 2020, visitors can explore the upper gallery which houses most of the Christian mosaics.</p>

<p>For nearly a thousand years this was the largest cathedral in the world, and the dome still dominates the skyline of the old city. The building's history is layered into its fabric: Byzantine mosaics, Ottoman medallions, and restoration work from several centuries sit side by side. The upper gallery, reached by a ramp rather than stairs, gives the best view of the dome and the surviving figurative mosaics.</p>

<p>Dress modestly — shoulders and knees covered, and women should carry a scarf. Entry is free, but the queues are long; late afternoon is usually the quietest window.</p>

<h2>3. Get lost in the bazaar district</h2>

<p>The Grand Bazaar is the best-known shopping destination, but don't miss the Spice Bazaar and the shops of Tahtakale. Also explore the arasta adjacent to the Blue Mosque for a memorable souvenir.</p>

<p>The Grand Bazaar is one of the oldest covered markets in the world, with thousands of shops arranged along dozens of vaulted streets. It is not a museum: the point is to wander, compare, drink tea with a shopkeeper, and bargain. Prices are negotiable, starting offers are rarely final, and the best purchases are usually textiles, ceramics, copperware, and lamps rather than the souvenir trinkets near the main entrances.</p>

<p>The Spice Bazaar is smaller and more focused on food — dried fruit, spices, Turkish delight, and tea — while Tahtakale, the surrounding market quarter, is where locals shop for household goods and kitchenware. Bring cash and comfortable shoes.<img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignnone size-full wp-image-1083" src="/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/CantoiStock-91814755-RFC.avif" alt="" width="1920" height="1280" /></p>

<h2>4. Sail on Istanbul's ferries</h2>

<p>Boarding the city's famous ferries is a must-have experience. Bosphorus sightseeing cruises offer breathtaking views of the palaces and parks on both shores. The Princes' Islands, accessible by ferry, are an ideal escape.</p>

<p>The commuter ferries are the cheapest sightseeing in Istanbul: a crossing from Eminönü or Karaköy to Kadıköy or Üsküdar costs little and gives you the full skyline — mosques, towers, and bridges — from the water. Longer Bosphorus cruises run north towards the Black Sea, passing the wooden villas and Ottoman palaces that line the strait.</p>

<p>The Princes' Islands, a short ferry ride from the Asian side, are car-free and best explored by bicycle or on foot. Büyükada is the largest and busiest; Heybeliada and Burgazada are quieter and better for a lazy afternoon.</p>

<h2>5. Explore the Istanbul Archaeological Museums</h2>

<p>The Istanbul Archaeological Museums, recently renovated, feature an extraordinary collection of artifacts, including marble statues and sarcophagi. The Tiled Pavilion displays rich ceramics from Anatolia, Ottoman, and Seljuk periods.</p>

<p>The complex is made up of several buildings, and the highlight for most visitors is the Alexander Sarcophagus, one of the finest examples of Hellenistic sculpture anywhere. The museum's collection traces the region from ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt through classical Greece and Rome to the Ottoman period.</p>

<p>It sits within the Topkapı complex, so it is easy to combine with the palace. Allow an hour and a half, and go in the middle of the day when the outdoor sites are at their hottest.</p>

<h2>6. Enjoy Istanbul's nightlife</h2>

<p>Beyoğlu is famous for its traditional meyhanes where meze and rakı are served. The pubs of Beşiktaş and Kadıköy attract a lively young crowd, while the bars of Bebek, Arnavutköy, and Yeniköy are perfect for sipping cocktails along the Bosphorus.</p>

<p>A meyhane evening is a ritual rather than a meal: small plates arrive one after another, rakı is diluted with water, and the table stays for hours. İstiklal Avenue and the side streets around it are the historic heart of this scene, though many of the best places are now in Kadıköy, on the Asian side.</p>

<p>For something quieter, the waterfront villages north of the city centre are where locals go for a drink with a view. Reservations are advisable at weekends.</p>

<h2>7. Discover Turkish contemporary art</h2>

<p>The Beyoğlu district is home to cultural institutions like Istanbul Modern and the Pera Museum. Galleries and museums can also be found in Nişantaşı and Kadıköy, offering a dynamic art scene.</p>

<p>Istanbul Modern, on the Karaköy waterfront, focuses on Turkish art from the twentieth century onwards and has a good café with harbour views. The Pera Museum is smaller and stronger on Orientalist painting and Anatolian weights and measures. Both are useful rainy-day stops and are rarely crowded on weekday mornings.</p>

<p>Beyond the big institutions, Nişantaşı and Kadıköy have a dense network of commercial galleries that change exhibitions frequently.</p>

<h2>8. Relax in a Turkish bath</h2>

<p>Istanbul's hamams offer an immersion into Ottoman history and culture. The Kılıç Ali Paşa Hamamı, Zeyrek Çinili Hamam, and Ayasofya Hürrem Sultan Hamamı are particularly luxurious.</p>

<p>A traditional hamam visit involves a hot marble slab, a scrub with a coarse glove, a soap massage, and a long rinse. The historic baths are housed in buildings designed by Ottoman architects, often with domed ceilings pierced by star-shaped skylights. Kılıç Ali Paşa, near the Galata Bridge, is the most architecturally rewarding of the restored baths.</p>

<p>Book a slot rather than turning up, and expect the experience to take about an hour. It is one of the few "tourist" activities in Istanbul that is genuinely part of local life.</p>

<h2>9. Discover the trendy neighborhood of Kadıköy</h2>

<p>Kadıköy, on the Asian shore, is known for its lively cafes, bars, and boutiques. Visit the produce market, the mural paintings, and the house-museum of Turkish rocker Barış Manço.</p>

<p>The market area around Güneşlibahçe Sokak is the best place to graze: cheese sellers, pickle shops, fishmongers, and bakeries packed into a few streets. The surrounding neighbourhood has a strong independent culture — bookshops, record stores, and street art — and feels noticeably different from the historic peninsula.</p>

<p>The ferry from Eminönü or Karaköy takes about twenty minutes and is part of the experience. Go in the late afternoon and stay for dinner.</p>

<h2>10. Admire the mosaics of the Chora Church</h2>

<p>The Chora Church, famous for its 14th-century mosaics and frescoes, was reconverted into a mosque in 2020. Visitors can explore its magnificent Christian works of art.</p>

<p>The building is small but the decoration is among the finest surviving Byzantine work anywhere: golden mosaics covering the life of the Virgin and the life of Christ, arranged across the walls and ceilings of the inner and outer naves. It is a short taxi or bus ride from the old city, near the Edirnekapı city walls.</p>

<p>Because it is a working mosque, opening hours are built around prayer times; check before you go, and dress modestly.</p>

<h2>11. Taste the flavors of Istanbul</h2>

<p>Turkish cuisine is celebrated for its simple meze, austere kebaps, and unstructured salads. Don't miss a serpme kahvaltı breakfast or a meze dinner.</p>

<p>Breakfast is a serious meal here: a spread of cheeses, olives, tomatoes, cucumbers, honey, jams, eggs, and endless bread, usually with tea. Street food is equally worth your time — simit (the sesame ring), balık ekmek (grilled fish in bread) by the Galata Bridge, lahmacun, and midye dolma (stuffed mussels) sold from carts in the evening.</p>

<p>For dessert, look for baklava shops that sell by weight and künefe, a hot cheese pastry. Avoid the restaurants with picture menus directly facing the main squares; the better food is usually a street or two behind.<img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignnone size-full wp-image-1079" src="/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/shutterstock2451850507.avif" alt="" width="1920" height="1277" /></p>

<h2>12. Admire the architecture of the Süleymaniye Mosque</h2>

<p>Commissioned by Sultan Süleyman the Magnificent, this 16th-century mosque is a masterpiece by architect Mimar Sinan. The surrounding complex is serene, with trees, a lawn, and a cemetery.</p>

<p>Süleymaniye sits on the third hill of the old city and is the largest mosque in Istanbul. The interior is calmer and less ornamented than the Blue Mosque, and the courtyard gives one of the best views over the Golden Horn. The complex originally included a hospital, schools, a library, and a soup kitchen, which gives you a sense of how a mosque functioned as a whole neighbourhood institution.</p>

<p>It is a working mosque, so avoid prayer times and dress modestly. The nearby streets have some of the best cheap eateries in the city.</p>

<h2>13. Explore the Basilica Cistern</h2>

<p>This underground Byzantine cistern is famous for its columns and Medusa heads. It is one of the city's most beloved tourist attractions.</p>

<p>Built in the sixth century under Justinian, the cistern once stored water for the Great Palace. Its forest of columns — hundreds of them, recycled from older buildings — rises out of shallow water and is lit in a way that makes the space feel like an underground temple. The two Medusa heads at the far end are the most photographed detail.</p>

<p>It is cool and dim, which makes it a good stop in the middle of a hot day. Visits take about thirty minutes.</p>

<h2>14. Admire the tiles of the Blue Mosque</h2>

<p>The Blue Mosque, known for its Iznik tiles, impresses with its large courtyard and six minarets. For even more exquisite tiles, visit the small Rüstem Paşa Mosque.</p>

<p>Officially the Sultan Ahmed Mosque, it was built in the early seventeenth century and is still one of the most recognisable silhouettes in the city. The interior is covered in thousands of hand-painted Iznik tiles in shades of blue and green, and the light through the stained glass is what gave the building its popular name.</p>

<p>Rüstem Paşa Mosque, near the Spice Bazaar, is much smaller and easy to miss, but its tilework is considered even finer. Both are working mosques: cover shoulders and knees, remove shoes, and avoid prayer times.<img loading="lazy" decoding="async" class="alignnone size-full wp-image-1081" src="/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/GettyImages-168773648.avif" alt="" width="1920" height="1278" /></p>

<h2>15. Discover the opulence of Dolmabahçe Palace</h2>

<p>The Dolmabahçe Palace, with its crystal chandeliers, marble floors, and Bosphorus views, is an example of royal opulence.</p>

<p>Built in the nineteenth century as the sultans moved away from Topkapı, Dolmabahçe is European in style and almost absurd in its scale: the largest Bohemian crystal chandelier in the world hangs in the ceremonial hall, and the waterfront façade stretches for hundreds of metres along the Bosphorus. It was also the residence where Atatürk died in 1938, and his rooms are preserved as a memorial.</p>

<p>Visits are by guided tour and photography is restricted inside. It is a taxi or tram ride from the old city, and combines well with a walk along the Beşiktaş waterfront.</p>

<h2>16. Let your feet guide you</h2>

<p>Istanbul is a city to explore on foot, with its distinct neighborhoods and lively street life. Whether in Fener, Balat, or Arnavutköy, every corner of the city offers a unique experience.</p>

<p>Balat and Fener, along the Golden Horn, are the best areas for a slow walk: colourful Ottoman houses, churches, synagogues, and small cafés spread over steep streets. Arnavutköy, on the Bosphorus, is a village-like strip of wooden houses and seafood restaurants. Neither area has a single "sight"; the point is the walk itself.</p>

<p>Wear shoes with grip, carry water, and allow yourself to get lost. The most memorable parts of Istanbul are usually the ones you did not plan.</p>

<h2>Practical tips for visiting Istanbul</h2>

<p><strong>Getting around.</strong> Buy an Istanbulkart at any major tram or metro station; it works across trams, metro, buses, and ferries and is far cheaper than single tickets. The T1 tram connects most of the historic sites, and the ferries are both transport and sightseeing.</p>

<p><strong>When to go.</strong> April to May and September to October are the most comfortable months. July and August are hot and crowded, and winter is cold and wet, though the city is quieter and cheaper.</p>

<p><strong>Where to stay.</strong> Sultanahmet is best for a first visit and the historic sites; Beyoğlu is better for nightlife and restaurants; Kadıköy gives you a more local experience on the Asian side.</p>

<p><strong>Etiquette.</strong> Dress modestly for mosques, carry a scarf, and remove your shoes. Expect to bargain in the bazaars, and carry some cash for small shops and street food.</p>

<p>Istanbul is a city that rewards every visitor with its many layers of history, culture, and modern life. Enjoy every moment spent in this fascinating metropolis.</p>
