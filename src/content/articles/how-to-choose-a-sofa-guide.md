<p>A sofa is the most-used piece of furniture in most homes and the easiest one to buy badly. It is usually bought in a hurry, judged by colour, and expected to survive a decade of dinners, naps, spills and moving house. The difference between a sofa that lasts and one that sags is rarely visible from across a showroom.</p>

<p>These are the things worth checking before you pay.</p>

<h2>Measure the room and the door</h2>

<p>Start with two sets of measurements: the space the sofa will occupy, and the narrowest route into the room. A sofa that fits the wall but not the stairwell is a very expensive mistake. Measure the door width, the hallway, and any tight turn. If the legs unscrew, that helps; if not, check whether the frame can be tilted through.</p>

<p>As a guide, leave at least 45 centimetres of walking space around the main seating. In a small room, a two-seater that fits properly will feel better than a three-seater jammed against the radiator.</p>

<h2>Check the frame</h2>

<p>The frame determines whether the sofa stays square. Kiln-dried hardwood — beech, oak, ash — is the benchmark. Plywood can be acceptable in cheaper sofas if it is thick and well joined. Particleboard and MDF are a warning sign in a piece meant to last. Ask what the frame is made of, and if the answer is vague, assume the worst.</p>

<p>Look for corner blocks, dowels, or screws rather than staples alone. A good test is to lift one front corner of the sofa a few centimetres: if the other front corner lifts with it and the frame stays rigid, the construction is sound. If the sofa twists, walk away.</p>

<h2>Understand the filling</h2>

<p>Seat cushions are where sofas fail first. Foam is the most common filling; high-resilience foam holds up better than cheap foam, and a density figure above 30 kg/m³ is a reasonable sign. Feather-and-down cushions feel wonderful but need daily plumping and will flatten over time. A foam core wrapped in down or fibre is a practical compromise.</p>

<p>Ask whether the cushion covers are removable and washable. If they are not, a single spilled coffee defines the sofa's future.</p>

<h2>Choose fabric for your actual life</h2>

<p>There is no universally best upholstery fabric — only the right one for the household. Tight-weave performance fabrics and microfibre resist stains and are good with children and pets. Cotton and linen look beautiful but crease and stain more readily. Velvet is durable but shows marks and needs care. Leather is easy to wipe but can be cold and scratched.</p>

<p>Ask for a sample and take it home. Look at it in your own light, next to your floor and walls, and rub it between your fingers. A fabric that feels thin in the showroom will feel thinner after five years.</p>

<h2>Sit properly — for at least ten minutes</h2>

<p>Seat depth and height matter more than style. A deep sofa is comfortable for lounging but hard to get out of for anyone with knee problems. A shallow sofa looks neat but is uncomfortable for tall people. Seat height around 40–45 centimetres suits most adults; depth around 55–60 centimetres is a good middle ground.</p>

<p>Sit in the corner, not the middle. That is where the frame and filling get tested. Lean back, put your feet up, and imagine watching a two-hour film. If you are already shifting after five minutes, the sofa is wrong.</p>

<h2>Think about the long term</h2>

<p>A good sofa can be reupholstered; a cheap one usually cannot justify the cost. Removable covers, replaceable cushions, and a frame with a long warranty are all signs that the maker expects the piece to last. Ask about the warranty and what it actually covers — frame only, or frame and suspension?</p>

<p>Finally, choose a neutral shape you can live with. Trends in silhouette move faster than the sofa will. A clean-lined sofa in a fabric you love will still look right when the current colour of the year has gone.</p>
