<p>Every November, a certain kind of shopper discovers the same thing at the same moment: French retail does not treat Black Friday as a Friday. It treats it as a season. And few French retailers stretch the season further than <a href="https://action.metaffiliation.com/trk.php?mclic=P512D11582D1D135" target="_blank" rel="nofollow sponsored noopener">Sarenza</a>, the Paris-born shoe and clothing site that has been selling footwear online since before most people were comfortable buying footwear online.</p>

<p>When I opened Sarenza's <a href="https://action.metaffiliation.com/trk.php?mclic=P512D11582D1D135" target="_blank" rel="nofollow sponsored noopener">Black Friday hub</a> in the first week of November, the page was already live, already discounting, and already promising a date still three weeks away. The page title said it plainly: <em>Black Friday 2026 — meet us on 27 November</em>. The merchandise underneath it, meanwhile, was on sale right then. That gap between the announcement and the actual event is the first thing worth understanding about how this sale works, and it is where most foreign shoppers misread the calendar.</p>

<h2>What Sarenza Actually Is</h2>

<p>Sarenza launched in 2005 on what was, at the time, a genuinely reckless premise: selling shoes online to people who could not try them on. It worked, and the company spent the next two decades expanding from footwear into a full fashion offer — shoes, clothing, bags and accessories for women, men and children. It now describes itself as eight sites across 22 countries, with more than 750 brands and around 60,000 references.</p>

<p>Since 2022 it has belonged to the Beaumanoir group, one of France's larger fashion conglomerates. That matters for a practical reason: Sarenza is not a scrappy startup running a warehouse sale. It is an established French retailer with a distribution centre just outside Paris, a French customer-service team reachable six days a week, and a promise — 24-hour express delivery on weekday orders placed before mid-afternoon — that most European competitors cannot match.</p>

<p>It also runs a marketplace. Alongside the stock it ships itself, the site lists items sold by third-party sellers. That distinction runs through every promotion Sarenza runs, and it is the single most common reason a discount code fails at checkout.</p>

<h2>How the Black Friday Page Is Organised</h2>

<p>The hub is not a single landing page with a banner on top. It is a full catalogue with its own navigation. Across the top you get tabs for women, men, children, shoes, clothing, bags, accessories — and, unusually, a tab for brands, which lets you start from a label rather than a category. That last one is the most useful tab on the page if you already know what you want.</p>

<p>Inside, the filters are the ones you would expect from a serious retailer: size, brand, colour, current promotion, discount depth, price, and — this is the important one — seller. When I looked, the Black Friday section held roughly 25,700 products. That is not a curated edit. It is a chunk of the catalogue with sale flags attached, and it rewards the shopper who arrives with a plan rather than the one who scrolls.</p>

<p>The site's own editorial guidance says as much. Sarenza publishes a "how to prepare for Black Friday" page that reads less like marketing and more like a mild intervention: work out what is actually missing from your wardrobe first, check your sizes, think about Christmas gifts before the discounts, and do not buy in a hurry. It is unusual advice for a retailer to give in the middle of its own sale, and it is also correct.</p>

<h2>The Three Overlapping Sales</h2>

<p>Here is where the French approach differs from the American one. In the United States, Black Friday is a date. In France, it is a period — and Sarenza layers several promotions across it. When I checked, three were running at once.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>"Le mois du Black Friday" — up to 50% off.</strong> The site's own headline claim, valid from 4 November to 17 November 2026 on a selection of items, subject to stock. The "up to" is doing real work in that sentence: the maximum applies to specific pieces, not to the page as a whole. The terms also state that this offer cannot be combined with another promotion.</li>
<li><strong>The main event on 27 November.</strong> The hub advertises the actual Black Friday date as the moment to return. In practice this is where the deeper, shorter-lived deals tend to appear — and where the version of the page you bookmarked in early November may look different from the one you find on the day.</li>
<li><strong>An extra 11% with a code.</strong> A banner across the site advertises <em>−11% supplémentaire</em> on orders from €99 with the code SINGLE, applying to more than 50,000 products, and pointedly says it works "even on Black Friday" items. Read the conditions, though: that specific code is dated 8–11 November 2026, tied to France's Singles' Day promotion, and excludes marketplace items and non-participating brands. The banner's phrasing is more generous than the small print.</li>
</ul>

<p>That last point is the most useful thing I can tell you about French sale pages in general. The banner is written by the marketing team; the conditions page is written by the legal team; and the legal team is the one that decides whether your code works.</p>

<h2>Reading a Product Card the French Way</h2>

<p>Sarenza's listing cards are more informative than most, and once you know what the three price lines mean you can judge a deal in about two seconds.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The current price</strong> is the large number. Prices are in euros, tax included.</li>
<li><strong>Prix d'origine</strong> is the reference price — in practice, the supplier's recommended price — with the percentage reduction beside it.</li>
<li><strong>Dernier prix le plus bas</strong> is the lowest price the item has sold for in the last 30 days. This is a European transparency requirement, and it is the most honest number on the page. If an item is marked 40% off but the 30-day low is barely below today's price, you are looking at a permanent markdown dressed up as an event.</li>
</ul>

<p>The cards also carry badges: BLACK FRIDAY, sometimes an additional percentage, sometimes a "made in Europe" flag. And near the bottom of each listing, the seller is named. That is where you check whether an item is shipped by Sarenza itself or by a marketplace partner — which determines whether the promo code applies at all.</p>

<h2>What Is Actually Worth Buying</h2>

<p>The categories that carry the sale are the ones France is genuinely good at. Footwear dominates, as you would expect from a company built on shoes: ankle boots, leather boots, loafers, retro running shoes, and the kind of mid-priced leather moccasins that French brands do better than almost anyone. Coats and parkas appear heavily too — the sale lands at exactly the moment the weather turns, which is not an accident.</p>

<p>Brands on the sale page when I looked ranged from the predictable to the pleasantly specific: Lacoste and Levi's, Timberland and Dr. Martens, Asics, Puma, Reebok and Vans alongside French and European labels such as Georgia Rose, Rose et Martin and Made By. A few examples, purely to give you a sense of the price architecture: a Lacoste down jacket at €314.50 against a €370 reference price; Timberland lace-up boots at €184 against €230; a pair of Georgia Rose leather moccasins at €71.40 against €119; Puma Club II trainers at €45 against €60. Nothing here is free. The good discounts sit in the 20–40% band, on mid-priced leather and on last-season colourways.</p>

<p>What I would actually buy from a page like this: a pair of leather ankle boots you will wear four days a week from November to March, a coat in a colour that already works with your wardrobe, and children's shoes in the next size up. What I would not buy: anything you have not tried in that brand before, anything in a trend colour you cannot imagine wearing in February, and anything you are buying because the percentage is large. The discounts are real but they are not rare — Sarenza runs promotions most months, and the "month of Black Friday" framing is a commercial calendar, not a once-a-year emergency.</p>

<h2>Sizes, Language and Paying</h2>

<p>This is the part that trips up English-speaking shoppers, and the site does not go out of its way to help.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The site is in French.</strong> The URL structure gives it away — this is the France/French version of the store, prices in euros, sizes in EU sizing. The brand runs separate sites for other European markets, but the page this article links to is the French one.</li>
<li><strong>EU sizes are not UK or US sizes.</strong> A women's EU 38 is roughly a UK 5 and a US 7.5; men's sizing runs on a different offset again. Sarenza publishes a size guide, and the honest advice is to use it rather than converting in your head — fit varies by brand and by the shape of the shoe, which is exactly the problem physical shops solve and websites do not.</li>
<li><strong>Payment is straightforward.</strong> Cards, PayPal including a four-instalment option, Klarna, Apple Pay. Sarenza also gives €20 off a first order from €100 for newsletter sign-ups, restricted to items it sells itself — worth knowing, and not combinable with other offers.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Delivery: Where Foreign Shoppers Get Caught Out</h2>

<p>This is the single most important paragraph in this article, so I will be blunt. According to Sarenza's own terms, delivery is available <em>only within mainland France</em>. If you live in London, New York or Toronto and you are reading this on a laptop at home, you cannot simply check out and wait for a parcel.</p>

<p>For anyone with a French address, the delivery menu is good and cheap:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Express 24 hours:</strong> orders before 16:00, €4.90, free above €69. No Saturday delivery.</li>
<li><strong>Mondial Relay pickup point:</strong> two to three working days, €4.90, free above €69.</li>
<li><strong>Colissimo to your door:</strong> two to three days, €5.90, free above €69.</li>
<li><strong>Chronopost express:</strong> 24 hours for orders before 16:00 Monday to Thursday, €7.90.</li>
</ul>

<p>One small inconsistency worth flagging: Sarenza's "about us" page still says orders must be placed before 14:00 for the 24-hour service, while the terms say 16:00. Where the two disagree, the terms are the version that counts.</p>

<p>So how does a visitor use this? Two realistic routes. The first is to shop during a trip: order to a pickup point or a hotel that has agreed to receive parcels, and time the order so it lands while you are still in the country. A Mondial Relay point a few streets from your accommodation is usually the least friction. The second is to have a French address in your life — a friend, family, a work address — and to treat the sale as a reason to plan a handover rather than a reason to expect international shipping.</p>

<h2>Returns, and What the Fine Print Actually Says</h2>

<p>French consumer law gives you 14 days to withdraw from an online purchase, and Sarenza goes further than the legal minimum: 30 days from dispatch, with return shipping paid by the retailer. That is a genuinely good policy, and it is the reason the sizing problem above is survivable rather than fatal.</p>

<p>The conditions are the usual ones, and they are enforced: items must come back new, unworn and in their original box. During the gift season the window extends to 8 January, which matters if you are buying presents during the sale. Marketplace items are a separate conversation — the third-party seller sets some of those terms, and returns can be slower.</p>

<p>The other fine print worth reading before you commit to a €250 coat: the big percentage offers exclude marketplace stock and non-participating brands, and the "month of Black Friday" promotion cannot be stacked with another offer. If a code fails at checkout, this is almost always why.</p>

<h2>Who It Is For — and Who It Is Not</h2>

<p><strong>It suits:</strong> anyone with a French address who wants well-made leather footwear, boots and coats at 20–40% off, and who values 24-hour delivery and free returns enough to plan around them. It suits people who already know their size in a given brand, and people shopping for children, where the next-size-up purchase is low-risk. It suits the traveller who is in France in late November and would rather buy a real pair of winter boots than pack them.</p>

<p><strong>It does not suit:</strong> anyone expecting international shipping from the French storefront; anyone who needs the site in English and will not use a browser translator; anyone chasing the "up to 50%" headline as though it were an across-the-board discount; and anyone who wants to try three sizes and send two back from another country. The return policy is generous, but it is generous within France.</p>

<h2>What Could Be Better</h2>

<p><strong>What works:</strong> the price transparency is genuinely above average. Showing the 30-day low beside the discount is the kind of detail that separates a retailer from a discounter, and it makes the sale page more trustworthy than most of what passes for Black Friday advertising in Europe. The delivery pricing is honest — free above €69, with a clear threshold rather than a rotating set of conditions. And the filters, including seller, are better than the site's dated visual design would lead you to expect.</p>

<p><strong>Where it falls short:</strong> the marketing and the terms are telling slightly different stories, and shoppers pay for that gap. A banner saying an extra 11% applies "even on Black Friday" sits above a conditions page that dates the code to a four-day Singles' Day window; the "month of Black Friday" runs to 17 November while the hero banner asks you to come back on the 27th. For a first-time visitor — especially one translating the page — that is genuinely confusing. The site is also French-only at this address, with no English storefront, and the delivery footprint stops at the French border. And the visual design, while functional, feels a generation behind the marketplaces it competes with.</p>

<h2>Conclusion: A French Sale, Read Correctly</h2>

<p>Sarenza's Black Friday page is not a doorbuster event and it is not a marketing stunt. It is a well-stocked French shoe shop with a month-long promotional calendar layered on top, and it is at its best when you treat it that way: decide what you need, check the 30-day low to see whether the discount is real, confirm the seller is Sarenza itself, and make sure the address you are shipping to is in France.</p>

<p>The discounts worth having are in the 20–40% range on leather footwear and outerwear, on brands that have been in the catalogue for years. The headline number is a ceiling, the calendar is longer than it looks, and the code is more conditional than the banner suggests. Read the terms, buy the boots, and skip the rest.</p>
