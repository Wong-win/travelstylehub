<p>A good bedroom is not decorated so much as engineered. The goal is a room that helps you fall asleep and does not wake you up. That means less visual noise, warmer light, and a bed that is genuinely comfortable. Hotels understand this; most homes do not.</p>

<h2>Start with the bed, not the accessories</h2>

<p>The single most important purchase in a bedroom is the mattress, followed by the pillow. Everything else — throw cushions, artwork, a bench — is optional. Spend your budget on the things your body touches for eight hours a night, and economise on the rest.</p>

<p>A mattress should support your sleeping position and suit your body weight. Firmer is not automatically better, and a mattress that felt wonderful in a shop for two minutes may feel very different after a week. Look for a trial period and a return policy, and use it.</p>

<h2>Keep the palette quiet</h2>

<p>Bedrooms benefit from fewer visual decisions. Two or three related tones on the walls, bedding and curtains; one texture for warmth; one small accent. This is not about making the room bland. It is about removing the small jolts of colour and pattern that keep the eye moving when you are trying to switch off.</p>

<p>Muted greens, warm greys, soft blues and oatmeal all work well. Very dark walls can make a bedroom feel cocooning, provided the room has enough natural light during the day and warm bedside lamps at night.</p>

<h2>Layer the bedding</h2>

<p>Hotel beds feel good because of layers, not because of thread count alone. Start with a fitted sheet in a natural fibre, add a duvet with a breathable cover, and finish with a throw or blanket at the foot. Natural fibres — cotton, linen, wool — regulate temperature better than polyester and feel better against the skin.</p>

<p>Wash bedding weekly and rotate two sets so one is always clean. A spare set of pillowcases is a small luxury that makes the bed feel fresh between full washes.</p>

<h2>Control the light</h2>

<p>Blackout curtains or blinds are the most effective sleep upgrade after the mattress. They block early summer light and streetlamps, and they make the room feel more private. If you rent and cannot drill, a tension rod with blackout panels is a workable compromise.</p>

<p>For evening light, use two bedside lamps with warm bulbs rather than a central ceiling light. Avoid cool white and avoid any light source that shines directly into your eyes when you are lying down. A dimmer or a smart bulb that fades to amber is worth the small cost.</p>

<h2>Remove the screens and the clutter</h2>

<p>If possible, keep the phone out of arm's reach and the television out of the bedroom. This is not a moral instruction; it is a design decision. A screen on the wall becomes the focal point and changes the room's purpose. A pile of laundry on the chair does the same thing more cheaply.</p>

<p>Give every object a place. A small tray for jewellery, a hook for tomorrow's clothes, a lidded basket for anything that does not belong. Visual order is not about emptiness; it is about knowing where things go.</p>

<h2>The final test</h2>

<p>Stand in the doorway with the lights off. Is the room restful? Is there anything that would catch your eye or your foot in the dark? Fix that one thing. A calm bedroom is not the most decorated room in the house. It is the one that asks the least of you at the end of the day.</p>
